# Project Gutenberg

## Collected / origin

- Michael Hart began Project Gutenberg by entering the Declaration of Independence into a university computer.
- Volunteers and partner projects turned printed works into searchable, reusable text, with transcription and proofreading as part of the collection process.
- Long before “training corpus” became ordinary language, a library was becoming a directory of machine-readable files.

## Why it sticks

- A book stored as plain text can be searched, copied, remixed, and counted at a scale that a shelf cannot manage.
- It makes the transition from digitization as preservation to text as computational material feel concrete.
- It also supplies a useful contrast with web crawls: deliberate editorial selection versus capture at industrial scale.

## Caveat in the margin

- Public-domain status is jurisdiction-specific. A work available in one country may still have restrictions elsewhere.
- A digitized book has multiple layers of rights and labor: the original work, the scan or transcription, and the platform's terms.

## Thread it to

- [[10-common-crawl|Common Crawl]]: both make text available at scale, but through radically different collection logics.
- [[13-webtext|WebText / GPT-2]]: the later corpus is also a reading list, but one shaped by links and voting instead of librarians and volunteers.
- [[topics/sourcing-and-ethics|Sourcing, access and ethics]]: accessible does not automatically mean unrestricted reuse.
