# Iris

## Collected / origin

- Each specimen is reduced to four measurements: sepal length and width, petal length and width.
- Edgar Anderson collected the underlying flower data; R. A. Fisher used it in the statistical work that made the table famous.
- A small botanical record became a canonical classification exercise after travelling through teaching software, repositories, and tutorials.

## Why it sticks

- It is the friendliest possible first encounter with supervised learning: compact, legible, and easy to plot.
- The same table can introduce measurement, classes, decision boundaries, and the difference between a demonstration and a deployed system. K-Means, baby.
- Its long afterlife shows how benchmarks become cultural objects as well as technical ones.

## Caveat in the margin

- “Small” does not mean context-free. Dataset copies differ in formatting, labels, and stated provenance.
- The clean separation in this example should not make real-world classification look equally clean.
