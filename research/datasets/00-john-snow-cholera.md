# John Snow’s Cholera Map

## Collected / origin

- Deaths were gathered address by address during the Soho cholera outbreak, then placed against streets and local pumps.
- Snow's famous 1855 map was one artifact in a wider investigation: local observation, interviews, mortality records, and argument about how cholera travelled.
- The Broad Street pump becomes the visual center because the marks cluster around it, not because a map alone settles causation.

## Why it sticks

- Data, place, and public health suddenly occupy the same page.
- It is an early reminder that a table can hide a pattern that a spatial view makes difficult to ignore.
- The recurring move is still familiar: locate events, compare them with possible exposures, then ask what the geography is actually telling you.

## Caveat in the margin

- This is not a clean “one map changed everything” story. The outbreak was already declining when the pump handle was removed.
- A dot on the map stands for a person who died. The graphic is compelling because the loss is real, not because death is decorative data.

## Thread it to

- [[08-openstreetmap|OpenStreetMap]]: maps become living, collaborative data rather than a fixed argument on paper.
